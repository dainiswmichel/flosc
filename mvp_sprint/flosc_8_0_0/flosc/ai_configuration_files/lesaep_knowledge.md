## Section 1 — Product Overview

**LeSAEp** = Learn Excellent Standard American English Pronunciation.

LeSAEp is a structured pronunciation course that teaches learners the phonetic science behind
Standard American English sounds — not just the sounds themselves. Where most pronunciation
resources tell learners *what* to say, LeSAEp explains *why* sounds are produced the way they
are: which parts of the mouth move, where air flows, and what makes each sound distinct from
similar sounds. This physical understanding gives learners a permanent foundation they can
apply to any new word, not just the words practiced in exercises.

**Who it is for:** Non-native English speakers who want to develop a clear, confident Standard
American English accent. Learners range from beginners who are hearing these distinctions for
the first time to intermediate speakers who know *that* they make certain errors but don't know
*why* or how to fix them consistently.

**What makes it different:**
- Teaches the phonetic mechanism behind each sound, not just imitation
- Covers 71 lessons addressing the full Standard American English sound inventory
- Connects quiz results to specific lessons — the AI can reference a learner's weak sounds
  and point them to the exact lessons that address those sounds
- Uses IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet) notation as a precise reference system, with
  plain-language physical descriptions for every term

## Section 3 — Phonetic Terminology (For Teaching, Always With Plain-Language Translation)

These terms are NOT standard learner vocabulary. Most learners have never heard "bilabial"
or "labiodental." The framing below applies to every term in this section:

LeSAEp can teach these terms when they help explain a problem — but the AI must always follow
a technical term with a plain physical explanation of what it means in the mouth.

**The AI should:**
- Recognize these terms when a learner or teacher uses them
- Use them when they clarify a specific difficulty, followed immediately by a plain description
  of what to do physically
- Map them to the relevant lesson numbers when the catalog is available
- Not lead with jargon — start with the physical reality, add the term as a label

**Example:** A learner struggling with /v/ — "v is labiodental: rest your lower lip lightly
against the edge of your upper front teeth while air flows through." No one thinks of /m/ as
bilabial — but telling someone that /v/ is labiodental is genuinely useful when they're placing
it wrong.

### Place of Articulation

**Bilabial** — Both lips come together.
- Sounds: /p/, /b/, /m/, /w/
- Plain description: upper and lower lips press together (p, b, m) or round into a tight circle (w)

**Labiodental** — Lower lip touches upper front teeth.
- Sounds: /f/, /v/
- Plain description: rest the lower lip lightly against the cutting edge of the upper front teeth;
  /f/ is voiceless (no vibration), /v/ is voiced (you feel buzz)

**Dental** — Tongue tip touches or approaches upper front teeth.
- Sounds: /θ/ (thin), /ð/ (then)
- Plain description: tongue tip between the teeth or touching the back of upper front teeth;
  /θ/ is voiceless (like air hissing through a gap), /ð/ is voiced (buzz with teeth and tongue)

**Alveolar** — Tongue tip touches the ridge just behind the upper front teeth (alveolar ridge).
- Sounds: /t/, /d/, /n/, /s/, /z/, /l/, /ɹ/ (r)
- Plain description: the bumpy ridge you feel with your tongue just behind your upper teeth;
  most English consonants use this area

**Postalveolar** — Tongue blade just behind the alveolar ridge.
- Sounds: /ʃ/ (shoe), /ʒ/ (measure), /tʃ/ (church), /dʒ/ (judge)
- Plain description: tongue tip pulls slightly back from the ridge; lips often round slightly

**Palatal** — Tongue body rises toward the hard palate.
- Sounds: /j/ (yes)
- Plain description: tongue hump moves toward the roof of the mouth

**Velar** — Back of tongue rises to the soft palate (velum).
- Sounds: /k/, /ɡ/, /ŋ/ (sing)
- Plain description: back of the tongue presses up toward the back of the roof of the mouth

**Bilabial-velar** — Both lips round AND back of tongue rises to velum.
- Sounds: /w/
- Plain description: double articulation — lips round like a tight circle while tongue back
  raises; /w/ is a glide/semivowel

**Laryngeal / Glottal** — Constriction at the glottis (vocal cords).
- Sounds: /h/, /ʔ/ (glottal stop)
- Plain description: /h/ is just an open airstream from the throat with no mouth constriction;
  /ʔ/ is a hard stop made by briefly pressing the vocal cords together (the catch you feel in
  "uh-oh")

**Velarized (dark L)** — The tongue simultaneously raises at the back (velar) while making
the alveolar /l/ contact.
- Sounds: /ɫ/ (dark L) — the American English L in syllable-final position (ball, feel, milk)
- Plain description: say /l/ normally, then notice how the back of your tongue also rises;
  American English always darkens L at the end of syllables; this sounds muddy or "heavy" to
  non-native ears

### Manner of Articulation

**Plosive (Stop)** — Complete closure, then release of airflow.
- Sounds: /p/, /b/, /t/, /d/, /k/, /ɡ/
- Plain description: airflow is completely stopped, then released in a tiny burst; voiced stops
  (/b/, /d/, /ɡ/) also vibrate the cords during closure

**Nasal** — Airflow redirected through the nose.
- Sounds: /m/, /n/, /ŋ/
- Plain description: mouth closes (lips for /m/, tongue-tip for /n/, back of tongue for /ŋ/)
  but the velum drops and air flows out through the nose

**Fricative** — Turbulent airflow through a narrow gap.
- Sounds: /f/, /v/, /θ/, /ð/, /s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/, /h/
- Plain description: air is squeezed through a narrow constriction, creating hissing or buzzing;
  voiced fricatives (/v/, /ð/, /z/, /ʒ/) vibrate the cords

**Sibilant** — High-pitched fricatives with a sharp hissing quality.
- Sounds: /s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/, /tʃ/, /dʒ/
- Plain description: the tongue creates a groove that directs air toward the teeth, producing
  a sharp hiss; /s/ and /z/ are the most prominent sibilants in English

**Affricate** — Stop released into a fricative (two-part).
- Sounds: /tʃ/ (church), /dʒ/ (judge)
- Plain description: tongue closes fully like a stop, then instead of a clean burst it releases
  slowly into a fricative — you hear two-part sounds: t+sh and d+zh

**Approximant** — Articulator approaches another but doesn't create friction.
- Sounds: /ɹ/ (r), /j/ (y), /w/
- Plain description: tongue or lips get close to another surface but not close enough to create
  turbulence — the sound glides smoothly

**Lateral Approximant** — Air flows around the sides of the tongue.
- Sounds: /l/
- Plain description: tongue tip touches the alveolar ridge, blocking airflow in the center;
  air flows out around both sides of the tongue

**Flap / Tap** — Very brief contact, faster than a full stop.
- Sounds: /ɾ/ — the American English "t" and "d" between vowels (butter, ladder, water)
- Plain description: tongue tip briefly flaps against the alveolar ridge without full closure;
  sounds like a quick "d" or "r"; this is why "butter" sounds like "budder" in American English

**Glottal Stop** — Vocal cords press together completely.
- Sounds: /ʔ/
- Plain description: the catch in "uh-oh"; in American English this often replaces /t/ before
  /n/ in words like "button" (bu[ʔ]n) or at the start of stressed vowels

**Glide / Semivowel** — Vowel-like but used as consonants.
- Sounds: /w/, /j/
- Plain description: /w/ is like a brief /uː/ moving into the next vowel; /j/ is like a brief
  /iː/ moving into the next vowel; they don't hold a position, they glide

**Aspirated** — Puff of air follows the release of a voiceless stop.
- Sounds: /pʰ/, /tʰ/, /kʰ/ — in word-initial position (pin, tin, kin)
- Plain description: hold your hand in front of your mouth — you should feel a puff of air after
  p/t/k at the start of stressed syllables; non-native speakers often omit this aspiration,
  making them sound flat; after /s/ (spin, sting, skin) aspiration disappears

### Vowel Types

**Monophthong** — Single, steady vowel position.
- Examples: /iː/ (beat), /ɪ/ (bit), /ɛ/ (bet), /æ/ (bat), /ɑː/ (father), /ʌ/ (but),
  /ʊ/ (book), /uː/ (boot)
- Plain description: tongue position stays relatively stable throughout the vowel

**Diphthong** — Vowel that glides from one position to another.
- Examples: /eɪ/ (bait), /oʊ/ (boat), /aɪ/ (bite), /aʊ/ (bout), /ɔɪ/ (boy)
- Plain description: you can hear (and feel) the tongue or jaw moving during the vowel;
  both positions are part of the same syllable nucleus

**R-controlled vowel** — Vowel colored by the American /r/.
- Examples: /ɝː/ (bird, heard), /ɚ/ (butter), /ɑːr/ (car), /ɔːr/ (for), /ɪr/ (near),
  /ɛr/ (there), /ʊr/ (tour)
- Plain description: the tongue bunches up (or the tip curls back) during the vowel, creating
  the characteristic American "r-colored" quality; this is one of the hardest features for
  non-native speakers

**Schwa** — The reduced, unstressed central vowel.
- Symbol: /ə/
- Plain description: the most common vowel in English — the lazy "uh" in unstressed syllables
  (about, banana, the); reducing unstressed syllables to schwa is essential for natural rhythm

### Prosody and Suprasegmentals

**Word stress** — One syllable in each multi-syllable word receives greater prominence.
- Plain description: louder, longer, and often higher-pitched than unstressed syllables;
  non-native speakers often stress the wrong syllable (reCORD vs REcord)

**Sentence stress** — Key content words are stressed across the sentence.
- Plain description: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs carry stress; function words (the, a,
  to, of, for) are usually reduced; this is what gives English its rhythm

**Rhythm (stress-timed)** — English beats fall on stressed syllables, not every syllable.
- Plain description: unstressed syllables are compressed and sped up to fit between stressed
  beats; languages like Spanish are syllable-timed (every syllable equal); English timing
  sounds completely different

**Intonation** — Pitch patterns across phrases and sentences.
- Plain description: statements fall at the end; yes/no questions rise; information questions
  fall; lists have a pattern of rises then fall on the last item; incorrect intonation makes
  statements sound like questions and vice versa

**Linking** — Words connect across boundaries in fluent speech.
- Types: consonant-to-vowel linking (stop it → sto-pit), intrusive /r/ (saw a → saw-ra),
  consonant assimilation (did you → didja)
- Plain description: in natural speech, words run together; gaps between words are an accent
  marker that makes speech sound halting

**Yod-dropping** — Loss of /j/ after alveolar consonants in American English.
- Examples: tune → /tuːn/ (not /tjuːn/), new → /nuː/ (not /njuː/), student → /stuːdənt/
- Plain description: British English keeps the /j/ (tyoon, nyoo); General American drops it;
  this is a systematic difference, not an error

**Vocal fry** — Low, creaky register at the end of phrases.
- Plain description: the crackling sound at the bottom of the vocal range; common in casual
  American English; often misread as boredom or disengagement by non-native listeners

**Dark L** — See *Velarized* above.

**Aspiration** — See *Aspirated* above.

### Vowel Mergers (American English Regional Variation)

These are sound distinctions that have merged (become identical) in many American dialects.
Learners from non-merged dialects may hear a distinction where most Americans don't make one.

**Cot-caught merger** — /ɑː/ and /ɔː/ sound identical.
- Most common in: Western US, most of Canada, large parts of the Midwest
- Example: cot and caught sound the same; don and dawn sound the same
- Note: Most General American speakers have this merger; non-merged speakers (Northeast, South)
  maintain a distinction

**Pin-pen merger** — /ɪ/ and /ɛ/ merge before nasal consonants.
- Most common in: Southern US
- Example: pin and pen sound the same; him and hem sound the same

**Mary-merry-marry merger** — Three vowels merge before /r/.
- Most common in: most of the US
- Example: Mary, merry, and marry all sound the same (/mɛri/)

**Fill-feel merger** — /ɪ/ and /iː/ merge before /l/.
- Most common in: Southern US
- Example: fill and feel sound the same

**Fell-fail merger** — /ɛ/ and /eɪ/ merge before /l/.
- Most common in: Southern US
- Example: fell and fail sound the same

**Salary-celery merger** — /æ/ and /ɛ/ merge before /l/.
- Most common in: parts of the Midwest and South
- Example: salary and celery sound the same

**Horse-hoarse merger** — /ɔːr/ and /oʊr/ merge.
- Most common in: most of the US
- Example: horse and hoarse sound the same; for and four sound the same

**Northern Cities Vowel Shift** — Systematic vowel rotation in Great Lakes cities.
- Cities: Chicago, Cleveland, Detroit, Milwaukee, Buffalo
- Pattern: /æ/ raises → /ɑ/ fronts → /ɔ/ lowers → /ɛ/ backs → cascade continues
- Plain description: a chain reaction where each vowel moves into the space left by the one
  that shifted before it; creates the distinctive Chicago/Cleveland sound

**Southern Vowel Shift** — Systematic rotation in Southern US.
- Pattern: /iː/ moves toward /eɪ/; /eɪ/ moves toward /aɪ/; low vowels shift and front
- Plain description: the opposite direction from the Northern Cities shift; creates the
  characteristic Southern drawl quality on front vowels

**California Vowel Shift** — Fronting and lowering in California.
- Pattern: back vowels move forward; /uː/ fronts; /oʊ/ fronts
- Plain description: common among younger speakers in California and the West Coast
